Correction and Corruption Signals: A Controlled Study of
Capability-Asymmetric Multi-Agent LLM Debate Under Adversarial
Wrong-Peer Exposure
Anonymous ACL submission

001

Abstract

002

In multi-agent debate, several large language
model (LLM) instances answer a question, read
one another’s answers, and revise. Reported
gains assume the agents are equally able. This
study asks what happens when the group is
unequal: one stronger model, the focal model,
debates weaker peers that argue confidently for
wrong answers. Eight focal models of widely
different ability are tested on MMLU-Pro and
GSM8K. All eight point estimates of the accuracy change under two confidently wrong peers
are non-negative, and the stronger models gain.
Two controls locate the cause: a re-answering
control shows the gain is not just a second
look at the question, and an honest-peer control
shows it is specific to confidently wrong peers.
The cost falls on the weak: the rate at which
a focal model drops a correct answer rises
sharply as its own ability falls. Accuracy alone
is misleading, though—reading an open-weight
model’s output distribution shows wrong peers
pulling probability mass onto their answer even
when the stated answer never changes. Two
findings caution against reassurance. Weak
peers that are wrong naturally flip the focal
model about twice as often as peers instructed
to be wrong, so adversarial framing understates
the risk; and a confidence-weighted filter still
fails once its design faults are repaired, because
weak peers sound equally confident when right
and wrong. All code, prompts, and trial logs
are released.

Collective reasoning in animal and human groups
is shaped by two competing forces: the value of
pooling evidence and the pressure to conform to
an apparent majority. Groups of large language
models (LLMs) face the same tension. In multiagent debate, several model instances first answer a
question on their own, then exchange their answers
and reasoning, then revise. When the agents are

equally able, this exchange tends to improve accuracy, because revision is driven by evidence rather
than by copying a peer (Du et al., 2024).

Real ensembles are rarely equal. We study the
unequal case with one stronger model, which we
call the focal model, and a set of weaker peers. We
track the focal model’s accuracy as it debates peers
that state wrong answers with confidence. Two
opposing effects are possible. The wrong answers
may pull the focal model along, an effect linked to
sycophancy (Sharma et al., 2024) and to the bandwagon effect under a unanimous majority (Asch,
1951; Koo et al., 2024). Or they may prompt the
focal model to reconsider its own answer, and so
to correct itself. Which effect wins, and for which
models, has not been measured under controlled
conditions. Confidence-weighted peer filtering has
been proposed as a fix, but has not been tested
when peers differ in ability.

046

This paper asks three questions. RQ1: does a
strong model keep its accuracy when its debate
partners are confidently wrong? RQ2: does the
answer depend on how strong the focal model is?
RQ3: can a model’s own confidence be used to
filter bad peers out of the debate?

062

To answer them, we run a controlled study on
questions from MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024)
and GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021), varying peer
composition while holding the debate structure
fixed. The weak peers are two small open models, Llama 3.1 8B (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and
Gemma 3 4B (Gemma Team et al., 2025), guided by
a persona prompt to argue for one assigned wrong
answer (Section 3.3). Their messages are therefore
confident distractors, not natural low-ability output;
an honest-peer control removes this framing (Section 4.2). Eight focal models of widely different
ability are tested, so that the effect of focal strength
is measured rather than assumed.

The closest prior work places a deliberately wrong
agent inside a debate. Amayuelas et al. (2024)
show that one adversarial agent, arguing persuasively for a wrong answer, can lower the accuracy of
a multi-agent system. A second line studies asymmetry between debaters and judges. Khan et al.
(2024) find that debate between two strong models
helps a weaker judge reach the truth, and Kenton
et al. (2024) test whether weak judges can oversee
stronger models. Smit et al. (2024) evaluate several debate protocols and report that the gains are
fragile and often no better than simple baselines,
which agrees with our result on homogeneous debate. These studies fix one focal strength, or vary
the judge rather than the answering agent, or hold
the number of wrong peers constant. This work instead varies peer count and focal strength together,
adds two causal controls, and measures the effect
across eight focal models.

3. An output-distribution probe beneath the
answer. Reading an open-weight focal
model’s output distribution shows wrong
peers moving probability mass onto their answer even on trials where the stated answer
never changes, so peer-induced movement occurs even in models whose accuracy appears
untouched (Section 4.8).

102

Adversarial and capability-asymmetric
debate

Debate as an error detector. A separate line
uses disagreement between models to find errors
rather than to raise accuracy. Cohen et al. (2023)
have one model cross-examine another and treat the
inconsistencies exposed as a factual-error signal,
which detects errors better than the model’s own
confidence does; Liang et al. (2024) use debate to
keep candidate answers apart rather than let agents
converge. The motivation for both is Huang et al.
(2024): without an external signal, models do not
reliably correct their own reasoning. Those works
treat a peer as an examiner whose disagreement
flags a possible error. We study the opposite case—
a peer that is confidently and demonstrably wrong—
and find the focal model’s accuracy still improves,
so the peer’s answer need not carry information
about which answer is right for exposure to help.

Du et al. (2024) introduced multi-agent debate and
showed that debate between homogeneous GPT3.5 instances improves factuality on arithmetic
and biographical tasks. Subsequent work qualified these gains: echo-chamber dynamics emerge
when agents share architectures or training distributions (Hong et al., 2025), and revision can
reinforce shared biases rather than correct them.

3.1

Experimental conditions

197

224

Debate evaluation is easily confounded, because
peer count, peer ability, and debate structure can
all vary at once. To isolate the effect of confidently
wrong peers, the debate structure is held fixed and
only the peers are varied. Every condition uses the
same two rounds: in Round 0 each agent answers
on its own; in Round 1 each agent sees the others’
Round 0 messages and gives a final answer. The
solo condition has no Round 1. Table 1 lists the
conditions.
Four conditions form the main ladder, from no
peers up to two confidently wrong peers (C1–C4);
seven further conditions are controls (Table 1).
C1R lets the focal model revise in Round 1 seeing only its own Round 0 answer, so any gain there
comes from a second attempt rather than peers.
C3H and C4H replace the wrong-answer persona
with the standard prompt, so the weak models answer naturally. C4split replaces one of the two
wrong-anchored peers with a correct-anchored one.
C2het replaces the two extra copies of the focal
model with two architecturally distinct strong models; because those are also weaker than the focal,
it varies diversity and peer strength together. C5R
and C5H apply the confidence filter, with wronganchored and honest peers respectively; legacy C5
is a diagnostic only, its peers never having been
asked for a confidence line.

3.3

Datasets and persona pipeline

299

Questions are drawn from two benchmarks.
MMLU-Pro (Wang et al., 2024) contributes 200
questions spanning ten subject categories (biology,
chemistry, computer science, economics, history,
law, mathematics, philosophy, physics, and psychology) with 20 questions per subject, stratified
50/50 into easier and harder halves by a zero-shot
accuracy probe. GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) contributes 100 grade-school mathematical word problems. The 300-question pool is answered five times
per condition. For the primary focal model this
gives 1,500 trials in each main condition (C1–C4)
and in each control (C1R, C4H); the filtered condition (C5) uses a 100-question subset with three
replications. The seven sweep models are each run
three times per question under the reduced protocol.
To check whether the GSM8K effect depends on
memorised problems, a contamination probe reuses the 100 GSM8K items with their numbers
changed and answers recomputed, then re-runs the
solo and two-wrong-peer conditions (Section 4.5).
Across all conditions and the eight focal models the
study comprises 39,470 API focal trials (37,500 on
the main 300-question pool, 970 on the perturbed
pool, and 1,000 the superseded 50-question crossvalidation subset), recorded as 179,145 individual
agent responses, plus 2,700 trials of the locally
served output-distribution probe (Section 4.8). A
trial is one focal-model episode; an agent response
is one model call within a trial, and the two are
not interchangeable. Each wrong-anchored weak
message uses one of four wrong-reasoning styles
(surface keyword match, false analogy, overconfident assertion, misapplied rule); details are in
Appendix S5. Persona assignment is randomised
over replications so that style does not confound
the condition comparison.

The filtered condition needs a way to decide, before deployment, whether a confidence threshold
can separate useful peers from harmful ones. A
common pre-flight test asks only whether wrong
peers are loud—whether they report high confidence often. That is not enough, and it is not even
the right direction. Our filter retains a peer whose
stated confidence is at least the threshold and drops
the rest, so it helps only when retention is more
likely for a correct peer than for a wrong one. The
quantity to test is therefore the retention gap

323

∆ret = P (retained | correct)−P (retained | wrong),

324

which is positive when the filter preferentially
keeps correct peers, and negative when it preferentially keeps wrong ones. We also report the area
under the ROC curve of confidence against correctness, with correct as the positive class.
An earlier version of the filter also had a separate
implementation fault: the wrong-answer persona
prompt did not ask the weak models for a confidence line, so they stated none in Round 0 and
every peer was removed before the filter could act.
We repaired this by adding the confidence line to
the persona prompt, so the filter is now tested on
peers that do state their confidence. The outcome
is reported in Section 4.4.

Metrics and statistical tests

Four primary metrics are computed for each condition and model. Round-0 and Round-1 accuracy is
the proportion of trials in which the focal agent’s
answer is correct. The flip rate C→I is the proportion of trials in which the focal agent was correct in
Round-0 but incorrect in Round-1—harmful revision. The flip rate I→C is the proportion in which
it was incorrect in Round-0 but correct in Round1—beneficial revision. Both are conditional on
the Round-0 answer. In the anchored conditions
every weak peer is wrong, but the two peers usually name different wrong answers, so “all peers
wrong” should not be read as “all peers endorse
one alternative” (Section 4.6 separates these). We
report these as revision rates and avoid labelling
them a conformity signature, since the design does
not isolate a social mechanism.

level on paired observations matched by (question,
replication index). The McNemar test (continuitycorrected) compares Round-1 correctness between

Two families are used. The primary contrast family
contains the eight contrasts in Table 3: the headline contrast (C4–C1), the four control contrasts
(C1R–C1, C4–C1R, C4–C4H, C4H–C1), the two

4.6 Replacing a wrong peer with a correct one

A natural reading of the harmful flips is social: the
peers agree, and the focal model goes along with
the majority. Our data do not support that reading at the level of the answer. The two anchored
peers in C4 draw their assigned wrong answer independently, so they name the same wrong answer in
only 16.4% of trials—and that makes no difference.

it gives no support for an answer-level bandwagon

The result runs against the simple prediction of
the debate literature. Du et al. (2024) report gains
from pooling correct arguments among equal peers,
while Koo et al. (2024); Sharma et al. (2024) document conformity when a model is told a wrong
position; one would expect confidently wrong peers
to lower accuracy. For strong focal models the opposite holds.

668

Two things follow that accuracy alone would
hide. First, improved accuracy and peer-induced
movement can coexist: the output-distribution
probe finds attraction toward the peers’ answer
even where the answer never changes, so what differs across models may be how much of that movement reaches the final answer rather than whether
it occurs. Second, the cost rose as focal ability fell
while the benefit did not track ability, so we would
not treat a mixed-ability ensemble as safe on focal
strength alone.

676

On mitigation, replacing one wrong peer with
a correct-anchored peer lowered harmful revision
and raised accuracy, whereas confidence-weighted
filtering did not: the weak peers we tested are retained by the filter almost regardless of whether
they are right. We do not conclude that confidence filtering is impossible in general, only that
self-reported confidence was insufficiently discriminative here.

Limitations

Ethical Considerations

744

697

Wrong-anchored peers are one part of the space.
The main conditions use weak peers guided to argue a fixed wrong answer, so they are confident distractors, not natural low-ability output. The honestpeer control covers the natural case and shows the
correction is specific to confidently wrong peers.
A matched observational comparison inside that
control associates naturally occurring weak-peer
errors with more harmful revision than anchored
ones (Section 4.2); because it conditions on peers
happening to err, it does not establish the direction
in which our anchored rates depart from deployment conditions. Both settings are stylised, and the
mixture between them is not swept here.

This study uses publicly available large language
models accessed via commercial APIs and publicly
available benchmark datasets (MMLU-Pro (Wang
et al., 2024), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021)). No
human subjects were involved and no personal data
were collected. The dumb-agent persona prompts
deliberately elicit adversarial wrong-answer responses; the prompts and generated responses are
confined to the experimental pipeline and are not
deployed or released as a stand-alone artefact intended for downstream use. The findings describe
a failure mode of confidence-based peer filtering
and should not be used to justify deployment of
mixed- capability ensembles in high-reliability settings without externally verified peer-quality signals. The compute footprint is modest: all debate
conditions run against commercial APIs for well
under $100 in total, and the only GPU component
is the output-distribution probe, a single 24 GB card
for under a day. No model training is performed
and the carbon impact is correspondingly small.

A second issue surfaced empirically only after
Stage 3. Dumb-agent Round-0 responses never
include a confidence integer (0 of 5,850 Round0 responses parse successfully), because the persona prompt does not instruct the agent to emit
one. The C5 filter operates on Round-0 confidences and therefore excludes every peer as
null/below-threshold; this is the proximate cause
of the 100% peer-message removal reported in
mitigation_summary.parquet. In Round 1,
where the debate context contains explicit confidence lines from smart-agent peers, dumb agents
emit a confidence integer in 78.7% of trials (4,606
of 5,850); this is the source of the 4,146-row substrate used in the gate computation above.
Both faults were then repaired. The persona
prompt was extended to ask for a confidence line,
so weak peers state a confidence in Round 0 as well,
and the filter was re-run on this corrected substrate
(C5R) and on the honest-peer condition (C5H).
Measured removal rates: legacy C5 removed 600
of 600 peer messages (100%); C5R removes 6 of
600 (1.0%); C5H removes 24 of 600 (4.0%). The
filter is therefore operating in C5R and C5H, and
does almost nothing, because confidence barely
separates right from wrong answers even when it
is present. The retention-gap test predicts this in
advance; the original loud-when-wrong test does
not.

The calibration gate was run between Stage 1 and
Stage 3 to predict whether confidence-weighted filtering would supply usable signal. The gate metric
is P (conf ≥ 60 | wrong, dumb agent, C3 or C4),
computed over 4,146 dumb-agent Round-1 responses with parsed confidence (Llama 3.1 8B Instruct: 2,162 responses, P = 0.9915; Gemma 3 4B
Instruct: 1,984 responses, P = 1.0000). The activation threshold for the gate as originally specified
was 0.40: the design assumed that if more than
40% of incorrect dumb-agent responses reported
high confidence, the high-confidence class would
be enriched for wrong answers and filtering would
have something to remove. The observed value
0.995 exceeded the threshold, so the gate passed
and Stage 3 proceeded.
In retrospect the gate metric as specified is misspecified for the filtering question, in two ways.
First, it measures loudness rather than discrimination: the partner rate P (conf ≥ 60 | correct) =
0.998 almost matches it, so confidence carries essentially no information separating correct from
incorrect dumb responses. Second, its sign does
not match the deployed filter. The filter retains
peers at or above the threshold, so the useful quantity is ∆ret = P (retained | correct) − P (retained |
wrong), which is +0.0036 here. A gate written
the other way round would treat “high confidence
marks a wrong answer” as the desirable case, when
for a retain-high filter that is precisely the harmful case. Either way the magnitude is far below
the 0.10 threshold, so the corrected gate fails and
Stage 3 would not have been run. Section 3.4 of
the main paper reports both the original gate result and the discriminative gap, and treats C5 as an

S5. Persona categories and validation

1130

1167

Four wrong-reasoning-style personas were used,
one per generated variant: surface keyword match
(answers based on superficial word overlap with
the question), false analogy (maps the question
to an unrelated but superficially similar scenario),
overconfident assertion (states the assigned wrong
answer with confidence and minimal justification), and misapplied rule (cites a real principle
or rule but applies it incorrectly). Each persona is
anchored to one specific wrong answer per trial,
sampled uniformly from the question’s wronganswer pool. Persona text was generated by a
dedicated pipeline (src/persona_generator.py)
using Llama 3.1 8B Instruct as the generation model at temperature 0.9 with a maximum of 350 output tokens. Generated personas were validated by a separate validation module (src/persona_validator.py) that checked
for the presence of a “Final answer:” marker,
agreement between the parsed final answer and
the assigned wrong answer, absence of hedging
phrases above a threshold count, and a length
between 30 and 1,500 characters. Of the 1,500
wrong-anchored personas, 1,116 passed on the
first attempt (74.4%), 370 passed after regeneration (24.7%), and 14 failed after the maximum
three regeneration attempts (0.9%); the 14 failures were replaced by sampling from the validated
pool, giving a retention rate of 99.1%. The correctanchored pool used by the split-peer condition was
generated and validated the same way, with the assigned answer set to the correct option instead of
a wrong one: 1,033 passed first (68.9%), 452 after
regeneration (30.1%), and 15 failed (1.0%), a retention rate of 99.0%. Both pools are released, and
the per-persona regeneration_attempts_used
and validation_pass_status columns reproduce these counts.

S7. Dumb-agent baseline accuracy by
subject

1187

Dumb-agent Round-0 correctness is zero in 5,850
of 5,850 trials by design: each persona prompt
pre-assigns one specific wrong answer per trial
(Appendix S5), and the validation filter discards
any persona response whose extracted final answer
differs from the assigned wrong answer. Dumbagent Round-1 correctness rises to a mean of 59%
for Llama 3.1 8B Instruct (3,170 of 5,466 R1 responses correct across C3, C4, C5) and 63% for
Gemma 3 4B Instruct (3,438 of 5,466 R1 responses
correct). This Round-1 rise reflects in-context anchoring drift: dumb agents see smart-peer Round-0
answers in the debate context and revise toward
them. Per-condition Round-1 dumb correctness:
69.7% in C3 (Gemma) and 64.8% (Llama); 60.2%
in C4 (Gemma) and 55.0% (Llama); 65.0% in C5
(Gemma) and 59.7% (Llama). The honest-peer
conditions measure the same two models without
any anchoring, and there their Round-0 accuracy on
the same pool is 50.4% (Llama 3.1 8B, n = 2,244)
and 45.1% (Gemma 3 4B, n = 2,256)—close to
the solo accuracy each reaches when used as the focal model in the sweep (50.2% and 44.9%; Table 4).
The wrong-anchored persona therefore moves these
peers from roughly coin-flip accuracy to zero by
construction, which is the size of the manipulation
the anchored conditions apply.

Two auxiliary analyses sharpen the interpretation
of the headline results in Section 4.1: Cohen’s κ
on Round-0 correctness across smart-agent pairs
(which quantifies whether peer samples are statistically independent), and AUROC of self-reported
confidence against per-trial correctness (which
checks whether confidence is usable as a peerquality score).

1348

Inter-agent κ. Cohen’s κ on Round-0 correctness, computed pairwise within each condition over
the same (question, replication) trials, quantifies
whether peer agents supply independent or correlated signal about question correctness. In C2
(three DeepSeek-chat instances), the three pairwise
κ values are tightly clustered around 0.72: 0.73,
0.72, 0.71 (95% bootstrap CIs all contained in
[0.66, 0.77]). For the GPT-4o-mini cross-validation
the three values are 0.78, 0.74, 0.75 (95% CIs
in [0.65, 0.85], n = 250 trials each). Three
temperature-0.7 samples from one strong model
produce highly correlated error patterns; C2’s
“three independent agents” are not statistically independent. Under the correlated-rater approximation
ESS = k/[1 + (k − 1)ρ] with k = 3 and ρ ≈ 0.72,
the three samples behave like ESS ≈ 1.2 effective
independent draws, which limits how much an SCstyle procedure can extract from C2’s Round-0 distribution. Pairwise κ values involving a dumb agent
are uninformative under this design because dumbagent Round-0 correctness is constant zero (the
wrong-anchored persona has no variance to correlate with); this limit is itself a design observation,
and the heterogeneous-smart control it motivated
has since been run (condition C2het, Section 4.1),
with its capability/diversity confound recorded in
Limitations.

Compute and cost. Every debate condition is
API-bound and needs no GPU. Stage 1 (C1–C4 for
