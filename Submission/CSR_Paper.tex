\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent debate frameworks let language models critique and revise one
another, with reported aggregate gains on reasoning tasks.
We measure what happens when one strong focal agent is exposed to a
controlled number of capability-weaker peers that have been adversarially
anchored to confidently-wrong personas (Section~\ref{sub:data}), in a
five-condition design across 7{,}300 trials on 300 \textit{MMLU-Pro}
and \textit{GSM8K} questions, using \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} as the
primary focal agent (1{,}500 trials per condition) and
\textit{GPT-4o-mini} as a cross-model comparison (250 trials per
condition).
The two focal models diverge under the same protocol:
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} Round~1 accuracy rises from 76.2\% solo to
84.1\% with two weak peers ($+$7.9~pp), while \textit{GPT-4o-mini} loses
4.0~pp under one weak peer.
The trial-level and question-level paired comparisons agree:
$33$ of $300$ items move from incorrect in C1 to correct in C4 against
only $12$ that move the other way (McNemar $p = 0.017$, Holm-corrected),
a $+7.0$~pp net question-level gain that mirrors the $+7.9$~pp
trial-level result.
A self-consistency-of-three baseline derived from C2's Round-0 samples
shows that the C2 lift is consistent with sample aggregation: the
$+1.9$~pp difference between C2's Round-1 accuracy and the SC-3
baseline is not statistically detectable at $n=1{,}500$
($p = 0.22$, Cohen's $h = 0.05$).
A confidence-weighted peer filter strips 100\% of peer messages: weak
agents emit no confidence integer in the persona-anchored Round-0 prompt
(0 of 5{,}850 Round-0 responses parse successfully), and in Round-1
responses where they do emit one, the conditional rates
$P(\text{conf}\geq 60 \mid \text{wrong}) = 0.995$ and
$P(\text{conf}\geq 60 \mid \text{correct}) = 0.998$ differ by less than
0.5~pp, so confidence cannot discriminate weak peers from correct ones
even when it is present.
The two focal models diverge on flip dynamics rather than on net
accuracy: \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} converts weak-peer exposure into
I$\rightarrow$C corrections ($55.8\%$ in C4 vs $27.5\%$ in C2),
while \textit{GPT-4o-mini}'s C$\rightarrow$I conformity-revision rate
rises threefold ($5.8\%$ to $17.2\%$, $p = 0.001$, $h = 0.30$) and
the I$\rightarrow$C and C$\rightarrow$I responses cancel in net
accuracy.
Confidence-based peer weighting is not a viable mitigation when peer
capability varies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Collective reasoning in animal and human groups is shaped by two competing
forces: the informational value of aggregated evidence and the social
pressure to conform to the apparent majority view.
Large language models (LLMs) deployed in multi-agent debate (MAD) frameworks
face an analogous tension.
In such systems, multiple model instances independently answer a question in
round zero, exchange their answers and reasoning, then revise in round one.
Evidence suggests that debate improves factuality when all agents are equally
capable, because revision is driven by genuine evidence rather than social
mirroring~\citep{du2023improving}.

When agents differ markedly in capability, a strong focal agent receives,
in Round~1, a mixture of correct and incorrect peer reasoning, with the
incorrect fraction increasing as more weak agents participate.
\textit{Sycophancy}~\citep{sharma2023towards}---a model's tendency to
agree with stated positions regardless of correctness---and the
\textit{bandwagon effect}~\citep{koo2024benchmarking,asch1951effects,bond1996culture}---conformity
pressure exerted by an unanimous majority---have been observed in
single-agent settings.
Whether they compound in heterogeneous multi-agent debate with
systematically varied peer composition has not been measured under
controlled conditions.

Existing work has either studied homogeneous debate (all agents of equal
capability), evaluated conformity in single-agent contexts, or examined
post-hoc emergent effects without experimental control.
A gap therefore exists in the understanding of how capability asymmetry
scales with peer count, and whether the underlying flip mechanism---a
previously correct answer becoming incorrect after peer exposure---is
moderated by focal-model strength.
Furthermore, confidence-weighted peer filtering has been proposed as a
practical mitigation but has not been empirically evaluated under
capability-asymmetric multi-agent conditions.

This study fills that gap through a fully instrumented five-condition
experiment run on 300 \textit{MMLU-Pro}~\citep{wang2024mmlupro} and
\textit{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021training} questions, with five replications
per question, covering 7{,}300 trials across five conditions.
The primary focal agent is \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}; the weak peers
are \textit{Llama~3.1~8B Instruct}~\citep{meta2024llama3} and
\textit{Gemma~3~4B Instruct}~\citep{gemma2025technical}, anchored to
assigned wrong answers via a persona pipeline
(Section~\ref{sub:data}) so that their Round-0 responses act as
\textbf{adversarial distractors} rather than as natural low-capability
outputs.
We use the phrase ``weak peers'' for brevity throughout, but the
findings transfer to debate with deliberately-wrong distractor peers
constructed from weak models, not to debate with honest weak peers
in the wild.
A three-stage judge cascade resolves ambiguous answers.
The result is a high-fidelity measurement of how peer composition affects
answer quality, sycophantic drift, and conformity in a strong focal agent.

The specific contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A controlled three-point peer-count measurement of focal-agent
          accuracy and flip rates across 7{,}300 trials, with effect
          sizes, GEE-clustered logistic regression, and trial-level
          paired McNemar comparisons.
    \item Paired trial-level and question-level McNemar comparisons
          agreeing in direction and magnitude: for
          \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}, $33$ questions move from incorrect
          in C1 to correct in C4 against $12$ moving the other way
          (Holm-corrected $p = 0.017$), and $187$ paired trials improve
          against $68$ that regress; the aggregate metric does not
          hide a subset regression.
    \item A self-consistency-of-three baseline derived from C2's
          Round-0 samples that shows the C2 lift over C1 is consistent
          with sample aggregation at the current sample size; the
          $+1.9$~pp gap between C2's Round-1 accuracy and the SC-3
          baseline is not statistically detectable ($p = 0.22$,
          Cohen's $h = 0.05$).
    \item An empirical demonstration that confidence-weighted peer
          filtering is unavailable as a mitigation under capability
          asymmetry, with a calibration-gate pre-flight test that
          predicts the filter failure deterministically from weak-agent
          confidence distributions in C3 and C4.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

%% ── 2.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Multi-agent debate, sycophancy, and conformity bias}
\label{sub:mad}

Collaborative reasoning among LLM agents has attracted substantial interest
since \citet{du2023improving} showed that debate between homogeneous
GPT-3.5 instances improves factuality on arithmetic and biographical tasks.
Subsequent work has qualified these gains: echo-chamber dynamics emerge
when agents share architectures or training distributions~\citep{hong2025sycon},
and revision can reinforce shared biases rather than correct them.
Sycophancy---the tendency to revise correct answers toward a stated peer
position---was characterised at scale by \citet{sharma2023towards}, who
showed it is robust to prompting strategies and model scale.
\citet{echterhoff2024cognitive} extend the analysis to decision-making
contexts, demonstrating that conformity biases are elicited by both
explicit framing and implicit social cues.
\citet{koo2024benchmarking} construct the CoBBLEr benchmark of six
cognitive biases, showing that bandwagon susceptibility is measurable
across models and correlates only weakly with overall task accuracy.
Bandwagon susceptibility in human studies is known to be amplified by
unanimous majorities~\citep{asch1951effects,bond1996culture}, a pattern
the present experiment directly tests in LLMs.
Recent benchmarks such as BenchForm~\citep{weng2025benchform} and
SYCON-Bench~\citep{hong2025sycon} provide aggregate conformity scores but
do not control for peer capability or vary peer composition systematically.
In aggregate, these studies establish that debate quality depends on
capability homogeneity and the absence of systematic social-pressure
mechanisms; the present work extends this by independently varying the
count of weak peers.

\subsection{Emergent dynamics and mitigation strategies}
\label{sub:emergent}

Social dynamics among populations of language models can produce emergent
conventions not present in any individual model~\citep{ashery2025emergent},
suggesting that bias phenomena scale with group size.
Mitigation proposals include majority voting, confidence-weighted
aggregation, and source-aware filtering; the present work subjects
confidence weighting to a deliberate stress test in C5.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

%% ── 3.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Experimental conditions}
\label{sub:conditions}

Debate-based evaluation is typically confounded by the simultaneous
variation of peer count, peer capability, and debate structure.
To isolate the effect of weak-peer exposure, this experiment holds debate
structure constant and varies peer composition across five conditions.
Each condition is characterised by the tuple (smart agents, dumb agents,
filtering):

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{C1}: solo smart agent (no debate); solo baseline. \\
\textbf{C2}: three smart agents, zero dumb; homogeneous debate control. \\
\textbf{C3}: two smart and one dumb agent. \\
\textbf{C4}: one smart and two dumb agents. \\
\textbf{C5}: as C4, with confidence-weighted peer filtering applied.
\medskip

In C1 the focal agent answers independently; its Round~0 answer equals
its Round~1 answer by definition.
In C2--C5 all agents answer independently in Round~0, share their answers
and reasoning, then produce a final answer in Round~1.
In C5 a calibrated confidence threshold of 60 filters peer messages:
only messages whose stated confidence meets or exceeds that threshold
are shown to the focal agent in Round~1.

%% ── 3.2 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Models}
\label{sub:models}

The smart focal agent is the model served under the
\texttt{deepseek-chat} alias by the DeepSeek direct API at the time of
the experiment (UUID
\texttt{477ec5c4-65d1-474d-9000-88440be70ed5}, 2026-05-03 to
2026-05-04 UTC; recorded in
\texttt{experiment\_metadata.json}), referred to throughout this
manuscript as \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} for compactness.
The \texttt{deepseek-chat} alias is a moving pointer that resolves to
DeepSeek's current general-purpose model; the architecture is
described in the DeepSeek-V3 technical
report~\citep{deepseekai2024v3}.
Smart non-focal agents in C2 and C3 are also \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}
instances, ensuring that performance differences across conditions are
attributable to peer composition rather than to heterogeneity among the
smart agents.
Dumb agents are \textit{Llama~3.1~8B Instruct}~\citep{meta2024llama3}
and \textit{Gemma~3~4B Instruct}~\citep{gemma2025technical}, accessed via
OpenRouter.
Each dumb-agent Round-0 response is generated from a persona prompt that
pre-assigns one specific wrong answer per trial
(Section~\ref{sub:data}); as a consequence dumb-agent Round-0
correctness is zero by design in 5{,}850 of 5{,}850 trials, and
Round-0 dumb responses should be read as adversarial distractors
constructed from the wrong-answer pool of each question rather than as
naturalistic outputs of these models.
Round-1 dumb correctness rises to 55--70\% on average across the three
heterogeneous conditions, because dumb agents see smart-peer Round-0
answers in the debate context and revise toward them.
The unprompted accuracy of these two models on MMLU-Pro and GSM8K is
not measured in this study; published evaluations place both well below
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}, with capability gaps in the 30 to 40
percentage-point range~\citep{meta2024llama3,gemma2025technical}.
The cross-validation uses \textit{GPT-4o-mini}~\citep{openai2024gpt4o}
(model string \texttt{openai/gpt-4o-mini}, via OpenRouter) as the focal
agent on a 50-question subset, with five replications (250 trials per
condition).
The underlying architecture of \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} is described in
the \textit{DeepSeek-V3} technical report~\citep{deepseekai2024v3}.

%% ── 3.3 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Datasets and persona pipeline}
\label{sub:data}

Questions are drawn from two benchmarks.
\textit{MMLU-Pro}~\citep{wang2024mmlupro} contributes 200 questions spanning
ten subject categories (biology, chemistry, computer science, economics,
history, law, mathematics, philosophy, physics, and psychology) with
20 questions per subject, stratified 50/50 into easier and harder halves by
a zero-shot accuracy probe.
\textit{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021training} contributes 100 grade-school
mathematical word problems.
The resulting 300-question pool is answered five times independently per
condition for C1--C4, yielding 1{,}500 trials per condition for the
primary focal agent and 250 trials per condition (50-question subset
$\times$ 5 replications) for the cross-validation focal agent.
Condition C5 is restricted to a 100-question stratified subset with three
replications, giving 300 trials. The total trial count across all
conditions and both focal agents is 7{,}300.
Each dumb agent response embeds one of four wrong-reasoning style
personas (\textit{surface keyword match}, \textit{false analogy},
\textit{overconfident assertion}, \textit{misapplied rule}); generation
and validation details are given in Supplementary~S5.
Persona assignment is randomised over replications so that persona effects
do not confound the condition comparison.

%% ── 3.4 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Debate protocol}
\label{sub:protocol}

Each trial proceeds in two rounds.
In Round~0 all agents in the group answer the question independently,
producing an answer and a supporting rationale.
In Round~1 each agent receives the full Round~0 outputs (answers and
stated reasoning) of all other agents, then produces a final answer.
In C5 the focal agent additionally receives a filtered view: only peer
Round~0 messages with self-reported confidence of 60 or above are
included; messages with no parsed confidence are treated as below
threshold and excluded.

A calibration gate ran prior to C5 to check whether the confidence
values reported by weak peers in C3 and C4 could discriminate correct
from incorrect responses.
Across 4{,}146 dumb-agent Round-1 responses with parsed confidence, the
gate measured $P(\text{conf} \geq 60 \mid \text{wrong}) = 0.995$
(95\% CI [0.991, 0.998]) and $P(\text{conf} \geq 60 \mid \text{correct})
= 0.998$, so the discriminative quantity
$P(\text{loud}\mid\text{wrong}) - P(\text{loud}\mid\text{correct}) =
-0.003$.
The gate as originally specified passed because the absolute
loud-and-wrong rate (0.995) exceeds its activation threshold of 0.40, but
the near-zero gap between the two conditional rates predicts that a
confidence threshold cannot remove wrong peers selectively.
We treat the gate's pass-decision as a misspecification: an informative
filter requires a discriminative gap, not merely a high marginal rate.

The C5 run confirms the predicted null-effect and surfaces a second
design issue empirically.
The dumb-agent Round-0 persona prompt embeds an assigned wrong answer
and does not include the universal confidence-line instruction used in
all smart-agent prompts; consequently weak agents never emit a
confidence integer in Round~0 (0 of 5{,}850 Round-0 dumb responses parse
successfully).
In Round~1, where the debate context exhibits the format from smart-agent
peers, weak agents comply with the confidence instruction in 78.7\% of
trials (4{,}606 of 5{,}850), suggesting that confidence elicitation in
small instruction-tuned models depends on in-context format
demonstration rather than the instruction alone.
Because the C5 filter operates on Round-0 confidences, every peer is
excluded as below-threshold null and the focal agent receives the
empty-peer-messages branch described in Section~\ref{sub:conditions}.
C5 therefore reduces to a solo task in 100\% of trials and is best
interpreted as a joint demonstration that (i) the originally specified
gate metric is misspecified for confidence-based filtering, since it
does not test the discriminative quantity, and (ii) confidence-based
filtering further requires the filtering-round prompt to elicit
confidence, a prerequisite not satisfied by persona-anchored prompts.

%% ── 3.5 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Metrics and statistical tests}
\label{sub:stats}

Four primary metrics are computed for each condition and model.
\textit{Round~0 and Round~1 accuracy} is the proportion of trials in which
the focal agent's answer is correct.
The \textit{flip rate C$\rightarrow$I} is the proportion of trials in which
the focal agent was correct in Round~0 but incorrect in Round~1, capturing
harmful sycophantic revision.
The \textit{flip rate I$\rightarrow$C} is the proportion of trials in which
the focal agent was incorrect in Round~0 but correct in Round~1, capturing
beneficial peer-induced correction.
Because all heterogeneous trials produced a unanimous-wrong dumb-peer
consensus, the C\textrightarrow{}I flip rate also serves as a conformity
signature; it is reported as the flip rate throughout and its bandwagon
interpretation is discussed in Section~5.

Statistical significance is assessed at the trial level on paired
observations matched by (question, replication index).
The McNemar test (continuity-corrected) compares Round-1 correctness
between condition pairs.
Holm-Bonferroni correction is applied within each focal-agent family of
six pairwise comparisons (C1 vs C2, C1 vs C3, C1 vs C4, C2 vs C3,
C2 vs C4, C3 vs C4); the C4-vs-C5 comparison is treated separately
because it uses a different (100-question, three-replication) substrate.
For C4 vs C5 the pairing is at the question level: C5's 3 replications
per question are reduced to a per-question majority vote, and C4's
5 replications on the same 100-question subset are reduced the same
way, after which the two condition-by-question vectors are paired by
\texttt{question\_identifier} for McNemar.
For all other pairs the pairing is at the trial level: each
$(\text{question\_identifier}, \text{trial\_replication\_index})$
pair appears once in each compared condition and is paired directly.
A generalised estimating equation (GEE) logistic regression with an
exchangeable correlation structure clustered by question identifier
fits Round-1 correctness as a function of weak-peer count (0 in C2,
1 in C3, 2 in C4); the GEE accounts for the within-question correlation
introduced by the five replications, which a naive logit would treat as
independent.
Effect sizes are reported as Cohen's $h$ for paired proportion
differences and as odds ratios with 95\% confidence intervals for the
GEE coefficient.
Post-hoc power is reported for any comparison whose raw $p$-value
exceeds 0.05.
All confidence intervals are 95\% bootstrap CIs (5{,}000 resamples)
unless otherwise noted.
Prompt templates and the answer-extraction cascade are documented in
Supplementary~S4.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
%% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

%% ── 4.1 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Accuracy and flip rates across conditions}
\label{sub:accuracy}

Accuracy and flip-rate outcomes across all five conditions and both focal
agents are reported in Table~\ref{tab:conditions}.
For \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}, Round~1 accuracy rises from 78.7\% in C2
to 78.3\% in C3 and then to 84.1\% in C4, against a solo baseline of 76.2\%
in C1.
A free self-consistency-of-three baseline (row SC3 in
Table~\ref{tab:conditions}) — per-trial majority vote of the three smart
agents' Round~0 answers in C2, before any debate exchange — attains 76.8\%
accuracy.
The $1.9$~pp gap between C2's Round~1 accuracy ($78.7\%$) and the SC-3
baseline ($76.8\%$) is not statistically detectable at this sample size
(two-proportion $z$-test, $p = 0.22$, Cohen's $h = 0.045$, post-hoc
power $0.23$, $95\%$ CI $[-1.1, +4.8]$~pp); the SC-3 baseline against
solo C1 is also not detectable ($+0.60$~pp, $p = 0.70$).
At $n = 1{,}500$ trials per condition this design cannot distinguish a
genuine debate-driven revision benefit from the gain attainable by
multi-sample aggregation alone.
Inter-agent Cohen's $\kappa$ on the three smart agents' Round-0
correctness is $0.72$ (Section~\ref{sub:dependence}); using the
correlated-rater approximation $\mathrm{ESS} = k / [1 + (k-1)\rho]$
gives an effective sample size of about $1.2$ independent draws,
which limits how much an SC-style procedure can extract from C2's
Round-0 distribution.
The much larger net gain in C4 ($+7.9$~pp at trial level, $+7.0$~pp at
question level) is driven almost entirely by the I$\rightarrow$C flip
rate, which jumps from $27.5\%$ in C2 to $55.8\%$ in C4: the weak
peers' Round-0 answers contain enough signal about the questions the
focal agent missed to produce a substantial correction effect, even
when many of the peers' own answers are wrong.
Paired trial-level and question-level McNemar tests both confirm the
C4 gain: at the question level, $33$ of $300$ items move from
incorrect in C1 to correct in C4 against only $12$ that move the
other way ($\chi^2 = 8.9$, $p = 0.0025$, Holm-corrected $p = 0.017$;
contingency $[[218, 12], [33, 37]]$ with rows indexing C1 status and
columns indexing C4 status, so the off-diagonal entry $b = 12$
counts regressions and $c = 33$ counts improvements); at the trial
level (paired by question and replication index), $187$ of $1{,}500$
trials improve against $68$ that regress (net $+119$ trials, McNemar
$p \ll 0.001$).
A separate analysis stratified by zero-shot probe difficulty shows
that the question-level gain is larger on probe-hard items ($+14$
questions net of $152$) than on probe-easy items ($+7$ of $148$),
consistent with the correction-signal interpretation.
The C$\rightarrow$I flip rate rises in the predicted direction from
5.0\% in C2 to 6.5\% in C4, but the effect size is small (Cohen's
$h = 0.05$ on the C2-vs-C4 contrast, two-proportion $p = 0.15$,
post-hoc power $0.30$); the gap is consistent with a monotone rise but
is underpowered for detection at the current sample size and should be
read as suggestive, not statistically established.
The corresponding I$\rightarrow$C flip rate change from 27.5\% in C2 to
55.8\% in C4 is large (Cohen's $h = 0.58$, $p < 0.001$, power
$> 0.99$), so the C4 net gain is dominated by the correction signal
even when the conformity signal is also active in the predicted
direction.

For \textit{GPT-4o-mini} the net Round-1 accuracy result is
inconclusive at the cross-validation sample size.
Round-1 accuracy falls from $64.8\%$ in C1 to $60.8\%$ in C3
($-4.0$~pp, bootstrap $95\%$ CI $[-12.4, +4.4]$~pp, $n = 250$
trials), and the C4 contrast is essentially null ($+0.4$~pp, CI
$[-8.0, +8.8]$~pp); both CIs overlap zero, so the accuracy-level
"opposite-signed outcome" is not statistically established for
\textit{GPT-4o-mini}.
The flip-rate response, by contrast, is unambiguous: the
C$\rightarrow$I flip rate rises from $5.8\%$ in C2 to $11.5\%$ in C3
and $17.2\%$ in C4 (C2-vs-C4 contrast: Cohen's $h = 0.30$,
$p = 0.001$, post-hoc power $0.91$), a roughly three-fold increase
in conformity-revision pressure relative to
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}.
The two focal models therefore diverge on flip dynamics rather than
on net accuracy: under maximum weak-peer exposure
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} converts most of its peer exposure into
beneficial I$\rightarrow$C flips, while \textit{GPT-4o-mini}'s
C$\rightarrow$I and I$\rightarrow$C rates rise in tandem and the net
cancels.
The confidence-weighted condition C5 yields accuracy $1.6$~pp below
C4 on the matched 100-question subset ($n = 300$ trials, $95\%$
bootstrap CI $[-7.5, +4.0]$~pp, $p = 0.453$ McNemar; the CI overlaps
zero by a wide margin and the null is far from definitive).
Every peer message is excluded by the Round-0 confidence filter in 100\%
of C5 trials (Section~\ref{sub:protocol}), so the contrast is between
C4 (one smart focal agent visible to two weak peers' Round-0 messages)
and a solo-equivalent setting.
C5 therefore neither validates nor refutes confidence-weighted filtering
as a mechanism; it documents that the protocol as configured strips its
own input.

\subsection{Peer-count trend}
\label{sub:doseresponse}

Figure~\ref{fig:doseresponse} shows Round~1 accuracy as a function of the
number of weak peers, with the C1 solo accuracy plotted as a horizontal
reference line.
The peer-count trend for \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} is non-monotone
in the accuracy dimension but strictly monotone in the
C$\rightarrow$I flip dimension.
A GEE logistic regression of Round~1 correctness on weak-peer count
across conditions C2--C4 ($n = 4{,}500$ trials nested in 300 question
clusters) yields an odds ratio of $1.19$ (95\% CI $[1.08, 1.31]$,
$p = 6 \times 10^{-4}$) for the per-peer increment for
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}; each additional weak peer multiplies the
odds of a correct Round-1 answer by 19\%, indicating that the
I$\rightarrow$C correction mechanism dominates for this model.
Question-level clustering moves the raw $p$-value from $1.68 \times
10^{-4}$ (naive logit) to $6 \times 10^{-4}$ (GEE), so the finding
survives proper accounting for within-question correlation.
The same regression for \textit{GPT-4o-mini} yields an odds ratio of
$0.97$ ($[0.80, 1.16]$, $p = 0.71$, $n = 750$): the point estimate is
below one, consistent with the C3 accuracy drop, but the cross-validation
sample is too small to establish a dose-response trend for this model.
Together, the two patterns indicate that the net accuracy outcome of peer
exposure depends on whether the focal model can evaluate peer reasoning
critically: a stronger model absorbs correct signal from even weak peers,
whereas a weaker model is overcome by the noise they introduce.

\subsection{Conformity signatures and a design limit}
\label{sub:asch}

The C$\rightarrow$I flip rate per condition measures the rate at which
a focal agent revises a correct Round-0 answer to an incorrect Round-1
answer after exposure to peer messages.
In every heterogeneous trial of this experiment the dumb-peer consensus
was unanimously wrong (the \texttt{dumb\_peer\_consensus\_status} field
in \texttt{final\_answers} records no split-peer cases), so the
C$\rightarrow$I rate measures the joint effect of (a) wrong peer answers
being present at all and (b) all visible peers being wrong
simultaneously.
The design does not separate these two factors, and as a consequence
the C$\rightarrow$I rate cannot be attributed exclusively to
unanimity-driven conformity pressure as distinct from generic
distractor-induced flipping.
A split-peer control (one wrong peer alongside one right peer) is the
required follow-up to make this distinction and is deferred to future
work.

Within this design limit, the flip rate rises with weak-peer count in
both focal models.
For \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} the rise from 6.0\% in C3 to 6.5\% in C4
is small and underpowered (Section~\ref{sub:accuracy}).
For \textit{GPT-4o-mini} the rise from 11.5\% in C3 to 17.2\% in C4 is
substantively large (Cohen's $h = 0.14$ on the C3-vs-C4 contrast,
$p = 0.12$, post-hoc power $0.34$), and the C2-vs-C4 contrast is
detectable at this sample size (Cohen's $h = 0.30$, $p = 0.001$,
power $0.91$).
The conformity-bias literature offers two related but non-isolable
mechanisms that could account for the rise: sycophancy
\citep{sharma2023towards} and the bandwagon effect under unanimous
majorities \citep{asch1951effects,bond1996culture,koo2024benchmarking}.
The present data are consistent with both being active but cannot
distinguish them.
Figure~\ref{fig:asch} displays the flip rate by condition for both
models.

The C$\rightarrow$I flip rate reported here is not directly comparable
to the conformity scores in existing benchmarks.
CoBBLEr~\citep{koo2024benchmarking} measures bandwagon susceptibility
by injecting a stated majority opinion into a single-agent prompt and
counting how often the agent changes its answer; the metric is a
single-agent susceptibility score, not a multi-agent debate outcome.
BENCHFORM~\citep{weng2025benchform} reports a debate-level conformity
rate but with a varying number of \emph{honestly answering} peers
rather than an adversarial wrong-anchored persona pipeline.
SYCON-Bench~\citep{hong2025sycon} measures sycophancy in multi-turn
user-model dialogues with explicit user pressure.
The present C$\rightarrow$I rate is therefore a debate-specific
operationalisation of revision pressure under adversarial wrong-peer
exposure, and a direct numerical comparison with these benchmarks
would require harmonising the peer-construction protocol; we report
the within-study peer-count gradient as the primary quantitative
contribution and leave cross-benchmark harmonisation to future work.

\subsection{Cross-model comparison and subject-level variation}
\label{sub:crossmodel}

The \textit{GPT-4o-mini} results confirm that the qualitative outcome
of weak-peer exposure depends on focal-model strength: Round~1 accuracy
falls 4.0~pp in C3 for the weaker focal model and the C$\rightarrow$I
flip rate reaches 17.2\% in C4, nearly three times the value for
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}.

The \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} headline gain in C4 is not uniform across
the question pool.
Split by source dataset, the C4 Round-1 accuracy is 79.9\% on the 200
MMLU-Pro multiple-choice items (vs.\ 76.0\% in C1, $+$3.9~pp) and 92.6\%
on the 100 GSM8K free-form math problems (vs.\ 76.6\% in C1, $+$16.0~pp).
The C4 gain is therefore concentrated on GSM8K: weak-peer messages on
numeric word problems supply the focal agent with alternative working
that can be re-checked arithmetically, even when the peers themselves
give wrong answers, while ten-option multiple choice gives a smaller
margin for the same mechanism.
Split by per-question difficulty (a zero-shot probe stratifies each
MMLU-Pro subject 50/50 into easier and harder halves), the C4 Round-1
accuracy reaches 92.2\% on probe-easy items ($+$6.0~pp over C1) and
76.3\% on probe-hard items ($+$9.9~pp over C1).
The larger gain on hard items reflects an I$\rightarrow$C flip rate of
16.7\% on probe-hard items versus 10.9\% on probe-easy items: weak-peer
exposure is most beneficial precisely where the focal agent's solo
accuracy is lowest, consistent with the correction-signal
interpretation in Section~\ref{sub:accuracy}.

The C$\rightarrow$I flip rate shows the complementary pattern.
The C4 C$\rightarrow$I rate is 6.4\% on MMLU-Pro items but only 1.8\%
on GSM8K items; weak-peer revision pressure operates most strongly
where the answer space is small and a wrong peer answer is structurally
plausible (one of ten options) rather than arithmetically refutable.
A direct probe of the recomputation behaviour supports this reading:
on $98.0\%$ of C4 GSM8K trials and $95.6\%$ of C4 MMLU-Pro trials the
focal agent's Round-1 answer does not match either peer's Round-0
answer (i.e.\ the focal produces a fresh answer rather than echoing a
peer), and within these fresh-recompute trials the focal-agent
accuracy is $94.5\%$ on GSM8K versus $83.6\%$ on MMLU-Pro---the
larger absolute accuracy and the larger gain in C4 on GSM8K reflect
that fresh recomputation on arithmetic problems yields ground truth,
whereas on multi-option MCQ the focal can still land on a wrong
option even when it ignores the peers.
Subject-level variation within MMLU-Pro is wide: physics and law show
C$\rightarrow$I flip rates above 20\% in C4, while biology and history
fall below 4\% (full breakdown in Supplementary~S8).
These subject-level differences are not predicted by the
correction/corruption decomposition in
Section~\ref{sub:accuracy} and motivate a follow-up study targeting
domain-specific vulnerability.

\subsection{Inter-agent dependence and confidence diagnostic}
\label{sub:dependence}

Two auxiliary analyses sharpen the interpretation of the headline results.

Cohen's $\kappa$ on Round-0 correctness, computed pairwise within each
condition over the same (question, replication) trials, quantifies whether
peer agents supply independent or correlated signal about question
correctness.
In C2 (three \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} instances), the three pairwise
values are tightly clustered around 0.72 (mean $\kappa = 0.722$ for
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}; $\kappa = 0.758$ for the \textit{GPT-4o-mini}
cross-validation), confirming that three temperature-0.7 samples from one
strong model produce highly correlated error patterns and that C2's
"three independent agents" are not statistically independent.
This is consistent with the modest SC3 gain over the C1 baseline reported
in Table~\ref{tab:conditions}: under the correlated-rater
approximation $\mathrm{ESS} = k / [1 + (k-1)\rho]$ with $k = 3$ and
$\rho \approx 0.72$, the three samples behave like
$\mathrm{ESS} \approx 1.2$ effective independent draws.
Pairwise $\kappa$ values involving a dumb agent are uninformative under
this design because dumb-agent Round-0 correctness is constant zero
(the wrong-anchored persona has no variance to correlate with);
this limit is itself a design observation and motivates a future
heterogeneous-smart-agent control.

AUROC of self-reported confidence against per-trial correctness checks
whether confidence is usable as a peer-quality score.
For the dumb models in Round-1, AUROC is $0.61$ (Gemma) and $0.65$
(Llama), with mean confidence on correct versus wrong responses of
$98.6/97.3$ and $96.5/92.3$ respectively---the gap between conditional
means is 1.3 to 4.2 points on a 0-to-100 scale.
For the smart focal agents, AUROC is $0.60$
(\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}~R1) and $0.67$ (\textit{GPT-4o-mini}~R1),
also near chance.
Confidence carries some weak signal across all models, but the gap
is too small to drive a binary filter at any threshold.
A direct test sweeps the confidence threshold from $0$ to $100$ in
steps of $5$ and records the maximum $F_1$ for predicting per-trial
correctness; for every (model, round) combination the improvement
over the always-predict-correct baseline is between $0.000$ and
$0.007$ (the largest improvement is $+0.007$ for
\textit{GPT-4o-mini} Round-0 at threshold $85$, against a baseline
$F_1$ of $0.779$).
No choice of threshold on these distributions yields a meaningful
separation between right and wrong responses, which is what the
calibration gate would have detected had it tested the discriminative
quantity rather than the absolute high-confidence rate
(Section~\ref{sub:protocol}).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

The results are best interpreted through two simultaneously active
mechanisms.
Peer exposure supplies a correction signal (I$\rightarrow$C flips) and a
corruption signal (C$\rightarrow$I flips) at the same time.
For a strong focal model exposed to weak peers, the correction signal is
large: weaker agents are wrong on different questions, so their collective
Round~0 answers contain information about the full question space, not
only about questions the focal agent has already answered correctly.
The I$\rightarrow$C flip rate of 55.8\% in C4 indicates that
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} extracts this signal efficiently.
The corruption signal, captured by the C$\rightarrow$I flip rate and its
monotone rise with peer count, operates simultaneously but is weaker in the
stronger model.
For \textit{GPT-4o-mini}, the corruption signal dominates: a
C$\rightarrow$I rate of 17.2\% in C4 represents a substantial fraction
of the model's correctly answered questions, and the net accuracy outcome
is neutral to negative.
The divergence between the two models is consistent with a
focal-strength-dependent threshold---a stronger focal model converts
wrong-peer exposure into corrections, while a weaker model balances
corrections against conformity-driven flips---but $n = 2$ focal models
is too narrow a basis from which to establish such a threshold. A
wider focal-model sweep is required to test this conjecture.
Confidence-weighted peer filtering is unavailable as a mitigation in
this setting.
Weak agents emit no confidence integer in the persona-anchored Round-0
prompt and produce non-discriminative confidence distributions in
Round-1 (Section~\ref{sub:protocol}, Section~\ref{sub:dependence}); a
filter operating on either round therefore lacks usable signal.
Mitigations that depend on externally verified peer accuracy, such as
held-out calibration sets or architecturally distinct verifier
models, are required.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ═════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

A controlled five-condition experiment with $7{,}300$ debate trials
measures how a strong focal agent's reasoning changes under increasing
adversarial wrong-peer exposure.
For \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}, the C4 condition (one focal, two
wrong-peer distractors) raises Round-1 accuracy by $+7.9$~pp at the
trial level and by $+7.0$~pp at the question level; trial-level and
question-level paired McNemar tests agree in direction and magnitude
($33$ questions move from incorrect in C1 to correct in C4 against
$12$ moving the other way; Holm-corrected $p = 0.017$).
A free self-consistency-of-three baseline derived from C2's Round-0
samples shows that the C2 lift over C1 is consistent with sample
aggregation: the $1.9$~pp gap between C2's Round-1 accuracy and the
SC-3 baseline is not statistically detectable at $n = 1{,}500$
($p = 0.22$, Cohen's $h = 0.05$).
For \textit{GPT-4o-mini} the cross-validation sample ($n = 250$ per
condition) is too small to establish a net accuracy effect (C3 vs C1
$\Delta = -4.0$~pp, $95\%$ CI $[-12.4, +4.4]$~pp), but the
C$\rightarrow$I flip rate rises significantly from $5.8\%$ in C2 to
$17.2\%$ in C4 (C2-vs-C4 contrast: $h = 0.30$, $p = 0.001$, power
$0.91$).
The two focal models therefore diverge on flip dynamics rather than
on net accuracy: \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} extracts I$\rightarrow$C
corrections from peer exposure ($55.8\%$ in C4 vs $27.5\%$ in C2),
while \textit{GPT-4o-mini}'s I$\rightarrow$C and C$\rightarrow$I rates
rise together and the net cancels.
C5 surfaces two methodological observations about confidence-based
filtering in mixed-capability ensembles.
First, weak agents do not emit a confidence integer under the
persona-anchored Round-0 prompt; in Round-1, after seeing the format
in smart-agent peer responses, they comply in 78.7\% of trials but
with confidence distributions that are statistically identical for
correct and incorrect answers
($P(\text{conf} \geq 60 \mid \text{wrong}) = 0.995$ vs
$P(\text{conf} \geq 60 \mid \text{correct}) = 0.998$).
Second, the originally designed calibration gate tested only the
absolute high-confidence rate against a 0.40 threshold and passed;
the discriminative gap relevant for filtering is $-0.003$, and a
better-specified gate must test the difference between the
right-and-loud and wrong-and-loud rates.

These findings have two implications for multi-agent debate deployments.
First, the net accuracy gain reported under wrong-peer exposure is a
function of focal-model capability: a stronger model can convert wrong
peer signals into useful corrections, but the conformity-revision rate
(C$\rightarrow$I) still rises monotonically with weak-peer count and
becomes the dominant outcome in a weaker focal model.
Both flip-direction rates should be reported alongside aggregate
accuracy to disclose this trade-off.
Second, confidence-based peer weighting is unavailable as a mitigation
in mixed-capability ensembles, because weak agents do not produce a
usable confidence signal: their persona-anchored Round-0 prompts elicit
no confidence integer, and their Round-1 confidence distributions are
statistically identical for correct and incorrect answers
(discriminative gap $-0.003$).
Mitigations that rely on externally verifiable peer accuracy, rather
than self-reported confidence, are required.
Future work should run a heterogeneous-smart-agent control and a
split-peer condition to separate debate-based revision from sample
aggregation and to isolate unanimity-driven conformity from generic
distractor pressure, and should extend the design to open-ended tasks
where binary correctness is not available.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{Self-consistency control is partial.}
The SC3 baseline reported in Table~\ref{tab:conditions} is derived from
C2's Round-0 samples, which share the C2 system prompt and its
\textit{Agent\_Alpha / Beta / Gamma} role markers.
A dedicated solo-sampling control with three temperature-0.7 draws
from the C1 prompt would close the comparison; that experiment is
deferred.

\paragraph{Homogeneous smart agents.}
All three smart agents in C2 (and the two smart agents in C3) are
\textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} instances; pairwise Cohen's $\kappa$ on
their Round-0 correctness is approximately $0.72$
(Section~\ref{sub:dependence}), so C2 conflates debate-based revision
with intra-model self-consistency on highly correlated samples.
A heterogeneous-smart-agent control with three architecturally
distinct focal models is the highest-priority follow-up.

\paragraph{Wrong-peer unanimity is not isolated.}
In every heterogeneous trial of this experiment the dumb-peer consensus
was unanimously wrong; the design does not separate unanimity-driven
conformity pressure from generic wrong-peer distractor effects
(Section~\ref{sub:asch}).
A split-peer condition (one wrong peer alongside one correct peer) is
required to make this distinction.

\paragraph{Persona-anchored dumb agents.}
Dumb-agent Round-0 responses are generated from a persona prompt that
pre-assigns one specific wrong answer per trial.
Their Round-0 correctness is therefore zero by design (0 of 5{,}850
trials), and their Round-0 messages should be read as adversarial
distractors rather than as low-capability outputs of these models.
The behavioural findings transfer to deployment settings in which
weak peers produce confidently wrong output; they do not transfer to
settings in which weak peers respond honestly without anchoring.

\paragraph{Two focal models.}
The opposite-signed-outcome finding rests on $n = 2$ focal models.
A wider focal-model sweep is required to establish whether the
qualitative outcome is predictable from any single capability metric.

\paragraph{Binary correctness only.}
The dependent variable is per-trial binary correctness on multiple-
choice and numeric-answer benchmarks.
Open-ended generation tasks have richer error structures that this
design does not address.

\paragraph{Benchmark contamination.}
\textit{MMLU-Pro} and \textit{GSM8K} appear in the pretraining corpora
of contemporary LLMs; per-condition accuracies should be read as
conditional on these specific item sets.
Constructed-from-scratch hold-out items would be required to rule out
contamination as a partial driver of the focal-agent baseline.

\paragraph{Behavioural, not mechanistic.}
Both focal models are accessed via commercial APIs; this study reports
the behavioural-level effect of peer composition on focal-agent
outcomes, not the representation-level mechanism.
Attribution to sycophancy or the bandwagon effect as named cognitive
processes requires either probing of open-weights focal models or the
split-peer design noted above.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
\label{sec:ethics}

This study uses publicly available large language models accessed via
commercial APIs and publicly available benchmark datasets
(\textit{MMLU-Pro}~\citep{wang2024mmlupro},
\textit{GSM8K}~\citep{cobbe2021training}).
No human subjects were involved and no personal data were collected.
The dumb-agent persona prompts deliberately elicit adversarial
wrong-answer responses; the prompts and generated responses are
confined to the experimental pipeline and are not deployed or released
as a stand-alone artefact intended for downstream use.
The findings describe a failure mode of confidence-based peer
filtering and should not be used to justify deployment of mixed-
capability ensembles in high-reliability settings without externally
verified peer-quality signals.
The compute footprint is API-based and modest (approximately
\$10--\$15 of inference cost across all conditions, no GPU training);
the carbon impact is correspondingly small.

\section*{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

The experiment pipeline, configuration files, parquet artefacts, and
the analysis scripts producing every numerical claim in this paper
will be released as an anonymised repository for the review period and
deposited at Zenodo upon acceptance.
The released artefacts include the per-trial log
(\texttt{trial\_log.parquet}, 35{,}050 rows), the consolidated
final-answers table (\texttt{final\_answers.parquet}, 7{,}300 rows),
the metrics summary, the statistical-test outputs, the
calibration-gate report, the question pool, the generated dumb-agent
personas, an \texttt{experiment\_metadata.json} listing actual served
model identifiers and package versions, and two analysis scripts
(\texttt{free\_analyses.py},
\texttt{inter\_agent\_dependence.py}) that produce every number in
this paper from those parquet files.

\paragraph{Models and snapshots.}
Smart focal: \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash} via the DeepSeek direct API
(runtime alias \texttt{deepseek-chat}).
Cross-validation focal: \textit{GPT-4o-mini} via OpenRouter
(\texttt{openai/gpt-4o-mini}).
Dumb agents: \textit{Llama~3.1~8B Instruct}
(\texttt{meta-llama/llama-3.1-8b-instruct}) and \textit{Gemma~3~4B
Instruct} (\texttt{google/gemma-3-4b-it}) via OpenRouter.
Judge cascade: \textit{Gemini~2.0~Flash} (primary, 4-key round-robin),
\textit{Mistral~Small} (secondary), \textit{DeepSeek-v4-flash}
(tertiary).
Model snapshots are aliases served by the respective APIs at the time
of the experiment (2026-05-03 to 2026-05-04 UTC);
\texttt{experiment\_metadata.json} records the served model strings
returned by each provider call.

\paragraph{Random seeds.}
The master seed is $20{,}260{,}502$.

are derived deterministically from the master seed, the question
index, and the trial-replication index; the mitigation-subset
selection seed is master seed $+1$.

\paragraph{Answer and confidence extraction.}
Regex captures 91.4\% of trial-log rows; a three-tier judge cascade
recovers 7.8\% more; 0.9\% are unrecovered parse failures.

flagged in the \texttt{confidence\_parse\_status} column of
\texttt{trial\_log.parquet}.
